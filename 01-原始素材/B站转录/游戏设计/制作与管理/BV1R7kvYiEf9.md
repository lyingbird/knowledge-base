OK, let's say you're making a 2D game and you need some kind of camera to frame the action. No problem, just pin the camera to the main character, right? Wrong. Do this, and you'll have a camera that's just really sharp, short sighted, and boringly static. So let's fix it. Here are five tips for making a much better 2D camera. First, let's talk about looking. Basically, if you keep the character in the middle of the screen all times, then you're dedicating just half of the display to the stuff in front of you. No surprise then why this happens often. So it might be better to focus more attention on the stuff that's coming up and ignore the stuff behind you. And there are a few ways to do it. The simple solution is to simply zoom the camera further. That means even if the hero is dead center on the screen, you can still get a good view of what's coming. A more involved solution though is to shift the camera in front of the character. If you can only move in one direction, it's enough to simply offset the camera from the main character. That's great for endless and alike. But if the player can travel both left and right, then you need to adjust the look ahead based on the direction of travel. So in cave story, there are two camera positions: one when you're moving left, and one when you're moving right. They both offset in front of the player, which is perfect for getting about shooting targets in front of you. When you swap direction, the game slowly slides to change from one position to the other. New Super Mario Bros. U does something similar. The camera is naturally biased on the right side of the screen, ahead of Mario. But if you start to backtrack for a short period of time, the camera will really catch up and swing further left of Mario. And in some generations, the camera reacts a little speed. It settles on the headshot when he's still, but moves further and further in front of him as he picks up and until he reaches maximum position. This is not just for platformers though. In hyperlifter, the camera is offset a few feet in front of the hero's head, so no matter which direction you're facing, the camera will frame what's coming up. And in levelers, the camera tracks in front of the player's plane, but only when the throttle is held down. That makes it possible to do sexy, stolen swinging shots without losing sight of your prey. It's even easier to do this if the player has a method for aiming, like in Nuclear Throne. Basically, ask yourself what does the player really need to see, and then make sure the camera isn't hiding it. Next up, let's talk about cameras and jumping. Because if you pin the camera to the character, then it's going to move the screen every time you jump. And if you're making a platformer genre, that's all about jumping, that's a lot of camera movement. This can make the player feel nauseous and make it hard to line up perfect jumps, especially if the platform disappears beneath the camera when you jump once. So there are two ways to help with this. One is to ignore jumps until the player reaches a certain elevation. In Donkey Kong Country Tropical Freeze, the camera doesn't react to the hills, ensuring that the ground stays in the same position at all times. However, when you land on a sufficiently high platform, the camera tracks up and sets a new baseline to work with. This means that the camera moves when you land it, rather than shifting mid-jump. The camera can break these rules in certain circumstances, like when you're climbing a rope or swimming. Now it moves up and down freely. Ramp legends is similar. The frame doesn't need to jump when ramp jumps. It can just track up in certain predetermined areas where the level goes beyond the frame. The other way to fix this is through the level design itself. In a game like Shuffle It, the levels are designed with maximum height, which just happens to be the exact height of the screen. This means that the camera only needs to track left and right, but never needs to move up or down, allowing the camera to stay still for jumps, go even further, and you can keep the entire level in a single screen, which means the camera doesn't need to move at all, which is perfect for precision jumping. It also helps to offset the camera vertically, so the hero is closer to the bottom of the screen. This gives more vertical space to play with and less need to move the camera up and down. But perhaps the best tip to remember is that you don't need to treat both camera axes equally. It can do completely different things when moving left to right, and when moving up and down. Next, let's talk about damping. If the camera perfectly tracks the character, then it's going to react to every single movement, no matter how small, and that can lead to camera that feels erratic and jerky, especially when making precision movements to line up perfect jumps. One solution is to use damping. Simply have the camera ease between its position and the character's position. This will make it follow events more smoothly and won't immediately respond to small shifts in position. Though beware, if the camera is slow to catch up to the player's movements, you can end up with moments where the character is actually in front of the camera, leaving even the screen real estate for looking ahead. Another solution is to pose some kind of dead zone. In this invisible window, where the character can wander around freely without the camera moving at all.
